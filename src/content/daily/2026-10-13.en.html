Setting the bed 安床 · Burial 安葬

On a Gēng Shēn day, Gēng metal sits at its own salary ground in Shēn — with the ninth month's Xū earth engendering metal, the metal qi is in season, at its post, pure and strong — while the Na Yin is Pomegranate Wood, wood conceived within metal, strength graced with refinement. Today the Golden Cabinet holds the Yellow Path, a star of fortune's storehouse presiding over blessing and abundance: marriages, business openings, trade and contracts all shine in its light. The Month's Grace, the King's Day and the Living Breath attend in support — a virtue of ceaseless growth answering prayers and prayers for heirs; the Post-Horse stands at the gate, so travel, relocation and moving into a new home ride the momentum, and with the Crying Hound's care, breaking earth and thanking the earth find their favor too. The inauspicious spirits — Yan Dui, Zhao Yao, the Five Departures and the Eight Unions — are listed, yet eleven auspicious spirits ring the day and they stand alone and weak. What alone is forbidden: setting the bed and burial — the Five Departures preside over parting, and both the rest of a bed and the rest of the grave are "rests" to avoid on such a day; in all else, the attending spirits carry the day.

Gēng's salary ground lies in Shēn: Monkey people carry their natal branch today while the day master stands at its full post, the salary spirit entering the chart — a sign of income and reward. Every money matter in the favored column — opening a business, trade, signing contracts, collecting wealth — is theirs to reap: negotiating terms and tallying inflows, this is the day to act with a free hand. The day clashes with the Tiger and the Sha lies South: those born in the Year of the Tiger should avoid long journeys toward the South and refrain from quarrels with Monkeys. Cold Dew has passed — warm days, cool nights; Monkey people would do well to sleep early, keep food and drink off the cold side, and nurture the gathering breath of autumn.

Metaphysics Insight

The most restless presence among today's auspicious spirits is Yì Mǎ 驿马, the Post-Horse. The name comes from the relay stations of old — horses changed mid-gallop, letters and dispatches passed hand to hand — for documents from every quarter, officials proceeding to their posts, merchants coming and going, all relied on the post-horse's run; so in fate-calculation it is taken as the spirit of movement. Its derivation is exact: look at the clash point of the day branch's triple harmony — for Shēn, Zǐ and Chén days the Horse dwells at Yín; for Yín, Wǔ and Xù days, at Shēn; for Sì, Yǒu and Chǒu days, at Hài; for Hài, Mǎo and Wèi days, at Sì. A triple harmony gathers one breath into a whole; a clash is that breath driven to its limit and bursting forth. The water trio of Shēn–Zǐ–Chén pools its qi at Zǐ — and Yín, which clashes Zǐ, strikes the spark: qi spurred into a gallop, a swift horse under the whip — this is the Post-Horse. So its whole meaning hangs on one word: to move. It presides over travel, relocation, moving house, taking up a post, transfers of office; with auspicious spirits beside it, motion carries joy — the honor of promotion, the smoothness of a far journey; hemmed in by dark spirits, it becomes instead the image of toil and restless running. In the old almanacs, an official departing for a post or a merchant setting out always consulted the Post-Horse first. Today it stands among the auspicious spirits, answering the favored column's travel, relocation and moving into a new home point by point — whatever needs moving, today is its hour.
