Xuánwǔ, the Dark Warrior, keeps the Black Path today, a star of shadow and private scheming; yet six auspicious spirits arrive together — Xiāngrì, Yìmǎ, Tiānhòu, Tiānwū, Fúdé and Yào'ān — and turn the fierce to the favorable: Yìmǎ, the Post Horse, governs movement and relocation, answering the favors for moving into a house, relocating, installing doors and renovation — an apt hour for changing homes and fresh paint; Fúdé and Yào'ān guard peace and blessing, Tiānwū furthers fortune, and the merchant's whole litany stands in the list — opening a business, trading, signing contracts, hanging plaques and collecting wealth — with contracts best signed to the North. The four inauspicious spirits each carry their counsel: Wǔxū, the Five Emptinesses, warns against wasteful spending from an emptying granary; Dàshà bars the stirring of earth, hence groundbreaking and breaking ground stand in the taboos; Chòngrì, the Repeated Day, forbids repeated rites, so funerals and burial are set aside — and curiously, betrothal gifts and pledging alliance stand among the favors while marriage ceremonies do not: today wills the engagement pledge, but the wedding procession must wait for another lucky date. Tomorrow brings the Autumn Equinox — day and night about to stand equal, metal at its height — so settle beds, move homes, open markets and sign contracts before the season turns.

Metaphysics Insight

Past today, tomorrow brings the Autumn Equinox. As the Chūnqiū Fánlù has it: "At the Autumn Equinox, yin and yang are half and half — day and night stand equal, cold and heat strike their balance." The sun crosses the equator, light and dark divide evenly, and autumn at last takes its place at the center. Of the twenty-four solar terms, the ancients weighed the two equinoxes and two solstices heaviest, and the imperial rites held that "in spring one greets the sun, in autumn one salutes the moon" — at dawn on the Spring Equinox the sun was honored in the east, and on the night of the Autumn Equinox the moon was offered sacrifice in the west; the Altar of the Moon outside Beijing's Fuchengmen, where the Ming and Qing emperors paid their dusk homage, still keeps that office. Here is the delightful part: the Autumn Equinox was originally the Moon Festival itself — for moon worship one naturally needs a moon worth worshiping, yet while the equinox holds nearly fixed on the solar calendar, its day within the eighth lunar month drifts year by year, and all too often no full moon rose for the rite. A moon sacrifice under a moonless sky was simply too dreary, so the worship drifted onward until it settled on the fifteenth of the eighth month, the night of the round moon — and that is how Mid-Autumn came to be the night of moon offerings. Today is the twelfth day of the eighth lunar month; three more days and the Harvest Moon fills. The almanac's list holds no "moon worship" among its favors, yet it brims with the homely business of setting beds, moving homes, collecting wealth and pledging alliance: the sacrifice belongs to the sky above, the living of days to the earth below — and in the glance down and the glance up lies the old Chinese art of turning the rhythm of heaven into the texture of the everyday.
