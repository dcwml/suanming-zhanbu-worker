On a Gēng Xū day, the Gēng metal sits upon Xū earth — earth begets metal and autumn metal holds command, hard, keen and clear; the Na Yin is Metal of the Hairpin and Bracelet, adornment metal that must be cut and polished before its beauty shows. The Month Virtue presides over the day, with Heavenly Grace, the Mother's Store, the Guarding Day, the Auspicious Date and the Continuation of Generations lending support — hence weddings, engagement pledges, betrothal gifts, relocation and moving house all stand favored, and sacrifices, prayers, travel, repairs and groundbreaking may proceed with confidence. Yet the Heavenly Prison holds the Black Path, with the Month Harm and the Blood Taboo at hand: anything drawing blood, acupuncture above all, is strictly to be avoided; felling trees and beam-making run against the keen edge of autumn metal; and funeral processions and burial stand against the life-renewing virtue of the Continuation of Generations — all set aside today.

Metaphysics Insight

At the head of today's auspicious column stands the Month Virtue (Yuè Dé) — the highest-ranking of all month-based spirits. Its derivation takes the heavenly stem from the month branch's trine: in the Yín-Wǔ-Xū months the virtue falls on Bǐng, in Shēn-Zǐ-Chén on Rén, in Hài-Mǎo-Wèi on Jiǎ, and in Sì-Yǒu-Chǒu on Gēng. This month is built on Yǒu, which belongs to the Sì-Yǒu-Chǒu metal trine, so the Month Virtue rests on Gēng — and today, a Gēng Xū day with Gēng as its stem, is precisely a day the Month Virtue descends upon. The old texts weigh it heavily: "where it governs, all evils melt away; on the day it holds, a hundred blessings gather" — like the clear radiance of the autumn moon, it softens the sharp edges of every hostile spirit. That explains today's seeming contradiction: the Heavenly Prison holds the Black Path and the Month Harm and Blood Taboo still stand in the inauspicious column, yet the almanac opens a whole row of glad occasions — weddings, engagement pledges, betrothal gifts, relocation, moving house. The taboos, in turn, each have their author: the Blood Taboo governs wounds that bleed, hence acupuncture is forbidden; the Continuation of Generations governs life renewed, hence funerals and burial give way. Virtue and harm sharing one ledger may look contradictory, but each governs its own affairs — the old almanac was never a simple good-or-bad label, but a running account of spirits checking and balancing one another.
