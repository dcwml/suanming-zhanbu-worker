On a Jǐ Hài day, Jǐ is yin earth — the earth of gardens and of sown fields; Hài is yin water, the last of the twelve branches — the water of early winter, of rain, frost and snow. Jǐ earth seated on Hài is earth in a land of water, damp soil holding water: a look that hides rather than shows, moistening rather than making noise. The Na Yin is still Wood on the Plain 平地木 — Wù Xū and Jǐ Hài share it: the wood of the open plain was told yesterday, and today is the last day of its term, for tomorrow's Zǐ hour brings Gēng Zǐ and the Na Yin turns to Earth on the Wall. The month pillar is Jǐ Hài and the day pillar is Jǐ Hài — stem and branch identical: when the day branch matches the month branch the day is a Jiàn 建, an Establishing day, when the month's commander holds power and nothing should be moved, only honored — the rule was told on the last Hài day, twelve days ago. The day god arrives at Heavenly Virtue 天德, a Yellow Path spirit of fortune: Heavenly Virtue is Heaven's sheltering grace, which wards off harm and ends trouble, and Bǎoguāng 宝光 in the auspicious list is simply another name for the Yellow Path's Heavenly Virtue; and the Moon's Virtue in Combination adds a further layer — in a Hài month the Moon's Virtue stands at Jiǎ, Jiǎ combines with Jǐ, and today's day stem is exactly Jǐ: the combination of the month's virtue alights on the day stem, a day where the month's noble qi gathers. Yet the four auspicious spirits are, to a one, spirits of "keeping" and "continuing": Wángrì 王日, the King's Day, is where the royal qi of winter stands, in Hài, favorable for offerings and for calling on the noble; Xùshì 续世 governs generations continuing and the incense unbroken; Bǎoguāng governs brightness — not one of them a spirit of founding or of movement. Ten baleful ones stand opposite, more than twice the auspicious count: Yuèjiàn 月建, Xiǎoshí 小时 and Tǔfǔ 土府 hold power together over the earth, and breaking ground and breaking soil stand avoided squarely upon them — the earth's qi is at its full, and to break the soil is to cross its edge; Yuèxíng 月刑, the Month's Punishment, attends too, and matters of injury are best avoided; Xuèjì 血忌 forbids the sight of blood, so needling, tooth-drawing and surgery wait a day; Jiǔkǎn 九坎 and Jiǔjiāo 九焦 govern pits and scorching, and heights, waters and boats go slow. Chóngrì 重日, the Repeated Day, asks the most care: the Sì and Hài days of every month are Repeated Days — the yin reaches its extreme at Hài, extremes turn, the end begins again, hence "repeated"; a Repeated Day avoids marriages, lest a good thing come twice, and avoids burial, lest a funeral repeat — the marriages and the burial in today's avoided list come from exactly this baleful one. With that, the five favorable matters read easily: clearing away misfortune 解除 — sweeping the house, dispelling what is unclean, clearing old debts and closing old matters; repairing walls 修饰垣墙 — patching and painting, the lightest kind of "moving," neither breaking soil nor troubling the earth's qi; coming-of-age ceremonies 冠笄 — the capping and the hairpin, putting one's person in order; travel 出行 — the road may be taken; and at the last, "all other matters set aside" 馀事勿取, which is today's whole rule. Opening a business is avoided as well: an Establishing day honors the old and does not acknowledge the new, and the work of setting up shop belongs to a day with more auspicious spirits than baleful. Clash the Snake, the Sha lies West: Sì and Hài stand opposed — with Snake people today, guard against contention above all; western journeys take a slower step. Minor Snow arrives tomorrow, and Hài is the last watch of the twelve branches — sweep the old dust and settle the old accounts today, and the life stored in the root will have somewhere to grow next year: that is the measure of a Jǐ Hài day.

Zodiac Fortune · Pig 猪

Today's earthly branch is Hài, which corresponds to the Pig — making the Pig the protagonist of the day: it is the Pig's natal day, with the Hài hours of nine to eleven at night on its watch, and the twelve branches have come round to their last — at tomorrow's Zǐ hour the round begins again from the top. Heavenly Virtue shines from the Yellow Path and the Moon's Virtue in Combination sits on the day stem, so the Pig's luck with benefactors runs highest today: knots long carried come loose with help — speak to an elder or a senior, and more often than not someone takes it up. In career, clearing away misfortune stands favorable: closing old cases, clearing the backlog, releasing an old agreement, letting go of what drags — anything with the sense of "settling" goes smoothly; repairing walls stands favorable too — patching, painting, changing a lamp, tidying the desk: small repairs may go forward freely, while a full renovation that breaks the ground waits. Coming-of-age ceremonies stand favorable: change the hairstyle, get new clothes, take the ID photo, put your appearance in order before you are seen; travel stands favorable as well — a short trip for work, visiting a client, going home for a look, all smooth on the road, only keep clear of the long haul due west. In wealth, the Wealth God sits North: not a day for opening a business or for large investment, but a good one for clearing accounts — calling in old debts, reconciling what is owed, gathering in money scattered outside; all other matters set aside, and keeping what you have is the day's income. In love, marriages stand avoided: registration, the banquet, the engagement — choose another day, for this is a Repeated Day, and a wedding above all shuns the word "twice"; those with a partner do well to loosen an old knot and talk out a misunderstanding that has gathered. In the body, the Blood Taboo stands in the list: needling, tooth-drawing, surgery and blood draws all wait a day; Jǐ earth answers to the spleen and stomach, Hài water to the kidneys and bladder — with Minor Snow coming, warm food and hot soup keep the middle burner fed, sleep early and rise late, and do not spend the yang. Clash the Snake, Sha West: Sì and Hài stand opposed — with Snake people, guard against contention and partnership above all today; western journeys take a slower step.

Metaphysics Insight

Yesterday the Dog of Xū told how Xū is a great axe; today the Pig of Hài takes up the graph Hài — and it is a root in the ground. The Shuowen Jiezi 说文解字 says: "Hài means gāi 荄, the grass root. In the tenth month the faint yang rises and meets the abundant yin." Gāi is the root of a plant. In the tenth month, the Hài month, the plants are withered and the leaves have gone back to the soil, and only the root still lives underground; the yang qi has not vanished — it has only withdrawn into the earth and hidden in the root, waiting for next year. The twelve branches reach Hài at the end of one round, and at the ending lies the beginning of the next: a month on comes Zǐ, of which the Shuowen says "the yang qi stirs and the ten thousand things sprout" — what the root holds is precisely that life, about to stir and not yet stirring. Why does Hài pair with the Pig? One account goes by the hours: nine to eleven at night are the Hài hours, the dark already deep and folk gone to bed, and the pig then sleeps its soundest — the saying has it that this is the hour it puts on flesh — so Hài was given to the Pig. Another account goes by the shape: in the oracle-bone script Hài hangs down in crooked, drooping strokes, and some scholars take it for a boar with lowered head and pricked ears, close kin to the graph for swine — Hài's Pig, on this view, comes from the written form. Both accounts stand, both are guesses, and the old script scholars have yet to close the case. Then a man of antiquity whose name carries Hài — Wáng Hǎi 王亥. He was a pre-dynastic lord of the Shang people, father of Shàngjiǎ Wēi, and it was Wáng Guówéi who found the man and the name in the oracle bones of Yīn; the Classic of Mountains and Seas, Great Wilderness East 山海经·大荒东经, records his end: "Wáng Hǎi lodged with Yǒuyì, and the River Lord [gave him] tamed oxen. Yǒuyì killed Wáng Hǎi and took the tamed oxen." To lodge is to sojourn; the tamed oxen are oxen broken to the yoke. Tradition has it that Wáng Hǎi broke oxen to the cart and, loading them with cattle, sheep, hides and silk, drove his herds to distant tribes to trade, exchanging the surplus of the fields for what his people lacked — the earliest merchant known to have traveled for trade. Lodged in the land of Yǒuyì, he was killed for his goods: the lord of Yǒuyì, it is said called Miánchén, took his wealth and his oxen. His son Shàngjiǎ Wēi later borrowed the army of the River Lord, destroyed Yǒuyì and avenged his father. The Book of Changes has the top line of the Wanderer hexagram, "he lost his oxen in Yì — inauspicious," and the fifth line of Great Strength, "he lost his sheep in Yì — no regret"; the modern scholar Gù Jiégāng argued that it is this old story they tell. Later ages honor Wáng Hǎi as the "First Merchant of China" — the word for merchant, it is said, comes down from this people who were skilled at trade. In today's almanac, travel stands favorable on a Hài day and opening a business stands avoided — the two halves of Wáng Hǎi's story: the road may be taken, the shop had better wait. Heavenly Virtue holds the Yellow Path and clearing away misfortune stands favorable: learn from the root under the earth, finish what needs finishing and sweep what needs sweeping; with the root kept safe, next year brings a tree of new branches.
