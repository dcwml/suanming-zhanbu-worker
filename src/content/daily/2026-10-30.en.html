Dīng fire sits upon Chǒu: yesterday Bǐng Zǐ hung the sun over rivers and lakes; today Dīng Chǒu lights the lamp in a cold hall — Bǐng is the sun's fire, Dīng the flame of lamp and stove, a difference of one word by which the light draws in from broad day beneath the eaves; Chǒu is the damp earth of the twelfth month that hides Xīn metal, and fire arriving there has its light dimmed by the earth and its flame folded in by the damp. Seven days past Frost's Descent with eight days yet to Start of Winter, the earth-qi of the Xū month holds the reins and the fire-qi gathers in by stages — the very order of deep autumn turning toward winter. The Black Tortoise holds the watch today, the Black Path staffing consecutive days — yesterday the Heavenly Prison left its gate ajar, today the Black Tortoise comes hair-loosed to the hall. Metaphysics Insight

Yesterday the Heavenly Prison stood in session; today the Black Tortoise comes to court — of the Black Path's six (Tiānxíng 天刑, Zhūquè 朱雀, Báihǔ 白虎, Tiānláo 天牢, Xuánwǔ 玄武 and Gōuchén 勾陈), the White Tiger and the Heavenly Prison have had their turns, and today arrives the one whose name rings loudest among the folk. Yet the name people know best belongs to the Black Tortoise's other title: the North of the Four Symbols. The ancients divided the stars along the ecliptic into twenty-eight mansions, seven to a quarter, each quarter guarding one direction — the Azure Dragon of the East, the White Tiger of the West, the Vermilion Bird of the South and the Black Tortoise of the North: the Four Symbols. The Black Tortoise's form is the strangest of the four: tortoise and serpent woven into one body, shell over back and coils entwined. Why "Wǔ," martial? The ancients' answer has charm: the tortoise bears scaled armor that can ward off assault, and to wear armor is to be martial. Why "Xuán," dark? Xuán is the color black, the color of the North — for the North belongs to water in the five phases and water's color is dark: the Black Tortoise is the water god of the North, governing winter and water. On the eaves-tiles of Han palaces, the Four Symbols riding clouds were already the star-totem the Chinese people knew best. The day-choosers took the Black Tortoise into the Black Path for a different layer of meaning: dwelling in the seat of the netherworld, it presides over private and murky affairs — on a Black Tortoise day of the Black Path, theft, spies and things done in darkness preside, so light-facing festive undertakings like marriages and opening a business must all give way; the avoided list's two entries today state exactly this. Curiously, of the four symbols the Black Tortoise rode the longest road from star to god: Daoism honored him as the Perfected Warrior, Zhenwu Dadi 真武大帝 — hair loosed, sword in hand, tortoise and serpent treading beneath his feet; the Song and Ming courts heaped titles upon him, and after taking the throne the Yongle Emperor rebuilt Wudang Mountain on a grand scale, until the guardian god of the North stood at last as the Demon-Subduing Heavenly Sovereign. One and the same god: in the almanac, a reminder to shun the murky; in the Daoist temple, a sword-bearing guardian of the northern wall — two faces of a single star, both written with the same character: uprightness. Today's Na Yin is Ravine Water — the water god keeping watch on a water day, one voice answering another; short as the favored list is, worship and road-mending stand clear — to keep still and upright is itself to follow heaven.
