Today is Dīng Mǎo 丁卯 — the stem Dīng is yin fire, the flame of a lamp or the warmth of a stove, luminous without blazing; the branch Mǎo is yin wood, the pliant timber of mid-spring, richest in the qi of sprouting growth. Mǎo wood feeds Dīng fire above it, stem and branch generating as one, and the Nayin is precisely Furnace Fire — yesterday's Bǐng Yín and today's Dīng Mǎo share this Nayin, Mǎo the firewood and Dīng the furnace flame, the very image of feeding the fire so it burns long and steady. The season stands in a Shēn month with Gēng metal in command, and fire qi should by rights be gathering in — yet today's auspicious spirits assemble in rare formation: in a Shēn month the Month Virtue stands in Rén, Rén combines with Dīng, and today's day stem sits squarely on the Month-Virtue Blend (Yuèdéhé 月德合), a virtue-star overhead that turns misfortune to fortune; Tiān'ēn 天恩 bestows grace from above, Yìhòu 益后 shelters offspring, Wǔhé 五合 governs the harmonious union of yin and yang, and Míngfèiduì 鸣吠对 favors burial — five auspicious spirits, each with its own charge, answering one to another the eighteen favorable matters, so that marriage, opening for business, moving house, relocating and travel all stand among the favors, a day of uncommon breadth for this stretch of the month. The presiding deity Zhūquè 朱雀, though of the Black Path and governing disputes of tongue and document, with Tiānlì 天吏, Zhìsǐ 致死 and Wǔxū 五虚 among the inauspicious spirits, is held well in check by the virtue-stars; one counsel only: signing contracts is favorable, but read every clause before the pen touches paper; and the unfavorable list — digging wells, installing a stove, breaking ground, opening ground for graves, digging ponds — is all work that digs deep and stirs the foundations, better deferred while metal qi stands strong and gathering.

Folklore

The deity presiding today is Zhūquè 朱雀, the Vermilion Bird. The Black Path gods were sketched some days back when Gōuchén 勾陈 held duty; today, let us trace the Vermilion Bird's far older lineage — it is one of the Four Symbols (Sìxiàng 四象) of ancient Chinese astronomy. Watching the band of stars along the way of the sun and planets, the ancients marked out twenty-eight lunar mansions, seven to each quarter under a guardian beast: the Azure Dragon of the East, the Black Tortoise of the North, the White Tiger of the West, and the Vermilion Bird of the South. The Vermilion Bird commands the southern mansions Jǐng 井, Guǐ 鬼, Liǔ 柳, Xīng 星, Zhāng 张, Yì 翼 and Zhěn 轸, linked star to star into the shape of a great winged bird; the south belongs to fire and its color is vermilion, hence its name in the family of phoenix-like birds, and in star-reading it presided over civilization, rites and music. Date-picking borrowed this southern bird into the roster of duty gods: a Zhūquè day is reckoned to the Black Path, after the nature of fire — flaring upward and quick to kindle strife — and what it governs is precisely the mouth and the pen: quarrels, document disputes, collisions of words. Today offers a living lesson: with the Vermilion Bird on duty the day should breed quarrels, yet Yuèdéhé 月德合 and Tiān'ēn 天恩 stand together, virtue-stars strong enough to check the baneful, and the favorable list runs to eighteen items — with only the counsel to read documents and contracts with care. From astronomy into star-reading, from star-reading into date-picking, the beasts of the Four Symbols wound up as duty gods of the almanac — the Vermilion Bird's story shows, layer upon layer, how the old almanac accumulated its world.
