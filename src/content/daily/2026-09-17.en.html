On Jiǎ Wǔ day, the day stem Jiǎ is the towering timber of beams and pillars, seated upon Wǔ Fire — wood generating fire, the very image of luminous wood and bright flame. The month pillar Dīng Yǒu arrives with Yǒu Metal commanding the season after White Dew, the direct-officer star in season, while Dīng Fire, the hurting-officer star, stands revealed on the month stem: autumn Metal refined by fire grows only nobler. Jīnkuì, the Golden Casket, presides on the Yellow Path, governing the treasury of stored wealth; joined by Bùjiàng, the auspicious spirit of marriage, and Fúshēng, who shepherds prayers and blessings, the "Yi" column opens with marriage ceremonies — coming-of-age rites, haircuts and gatherings with kin and friends all favored. Yet Tiānzéi, the Heavenly Thief, arrives alongside: a treasury should stay closed, hence the taboos on opening granaries, shipping out wealth and opening market; Jiǔkǎn and Jiǔjiāo, the Nine Ravines and Nine Scorchings, warn of flood and drought, barring planting, acquiring livestock, raising beams, roofing houses, breaking ground and reopening graves; and on a Jiǎ wood day the axe is ill received, so felling trees is also avoided. Overall, today favors gathering over dispersing, storing over exposing: ceremonies and blessings are timely, while ventures of spending and opening market should await a better day.

Metaphysics Insight

Among today's favors stands one of the oldest rites in the Chinese canon: Guānjī, the capping and hairpin ceremonies — the coming-of-age rites of old. The Book of Rites records that a man at twenty was capped: three times a cap was placed upon his head, first the black cloth cap, then the leather cap, then the ceremonial cap, while the address ran, "Forsake your youthful will and follow your matured virtue." A girl at fifteen underwent the hairpin rite, her hair coiled up and a hairpin driven through it — hence "arriving at the hairpin age." From that day one dressed and combed as an adult, and talk of marriage might begin. The ancients counted this among life's great ceremonies — the Guan Yi says, "The capping is the beginning of all rites": only after capping does one enter the ranks of the gentleman, and so the almanac keeps a Guānjī column of its own, to choose an auspicious day for it. "Haircut" in the old custom usually meant the shaving of an infant's fetal hair and the first growing of it — a rite of arranging the hair, hence it stands beside capping in today's favors. Today is Jiǎ Wǔ, and Jiǎ is the timber of pillars — a tree grown to maturity, the very image of coming of age; Jīnkuì, the Golden Casket, governs treasured things, and what are caps and hairpins but ornaments of gold and jade? That both stand in today's list is a footnote to the ancients' wisdom: rites perfect the person.
