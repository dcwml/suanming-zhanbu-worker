On an Xīn Chǒu day, Xīn is yin metal — the metal of pearls, jade, hairpin and bracelet; Chǒu is yin earth, the frozen soil of deep winter — Xīn metal seated on Chǒu, and Chǒu is the storehouse of metal among the twelve branches: pearls and jade laid in their casket, a look that sheathes its edge and quietly keeps what is true. The Na Yin is still Earth on the Wall 壁上土 — Gēng Zǐ and Xīn Chǒu share it, the wall's earth sheltering the room; Minor Snow has passed and the cold deepens by the day, and there is use for it. Today is the fifteenth of the tenth lunar month — the Lower Origin festival. The day god arrives at the Jade Hall 玉堂, a Yellow Path spirit of fortune: the Jade Hall is the name of a heavenly palace, governing documents, celebrations, feasting and gladness — of the six Yellow Path gods, the spirit of good cheer. Yet there are five auspicious spirits against six baleful ones — the baleful side is the larger — and the one at its head is of no small standing: Yuèyàn 月厌, the Month's Aversion. In a Hài month the Month's Aversion stands on Chǒu; yàn is the same character as in "aversion-quelling" 厌胜, to press down and subdue, and the Month's Aversion is among the most widely avoided of the month's baleful spirits — marriages, travel, entering a house, praying for blessings: most of today's avoided list comes from it, front to back. Hence today's uncommon picture: a Yellow Path lucky day on which the favorable list keeps only nine matters, while five great matters — opening a business, marriage, travel, entering a house and breaking ground — all go into the avoided column. The Jade Hall's luck is the luck of repose, not of movement: Shǒurì 守日, the Keeping Day, governs quiet keeping; Tiānwū 天巫 governs invocations and offerings; Fúdé 福德 governs blessings and feasting; Yùyǔ 玉宇 governs the peace of the house — the five together shelter one thing only: keeping. Within keeping there is still room: offerings and sacrifice 祭祀 — incense for the ancestors on the Lower Origin, exactly in season; sculpting and painting sacred images 塑绘 — coloring and gilding the figures; a haircut 理发 — the old hair gone, a new look about one; meeting family and friends 会亲友 — a small gathering round the stove; rearing livestock 牧养 and building livestock pens 造畜稠 — feeding the animals and mending the pens; digging a pond 开池 — opening a pool: taking water without stirring a foundation; hunting 畋猎 and weaving nets 结网 — the winter hunt and the nets of old, which today may be read as exercise out of doors and getting the gear in order. Digging earth is digging earth, yet digging a pond stands favorable and breaking ground stands avoided: one takes water, the other stirs a foundation — the almanac draws the line finely. The baleful ones each keep their own office, read by their images: Dìhuǒ 地火, the Earth Fire, governs the fire within the soil — breaking ground and building the stove are avoided; Jiǔkōng 九空, the Nine Voids, governs what is hollow and wasteful — opening a business and thanking the earth are avoided; Guījì 归忌, the Returning Taboo, stands against journeys that bode ill for the house — travel and entering a house are avoided; Dàshà 大煞 and Xínghěn 行狠 govern harsh and violent qi — raising the roof beam and burial are avoided. Clash the Sheep, the Sha lies East: Chǒu and Wèi stand opposed — with Sheep people today, guard against contention above all; eastern journeys take a slower step. The Jade Hall holds the day and the Month's Aversion presses overhead; today's measure is this: honor what should be honored, gather with whom you should gather, keep what should be kept — choose another day for the great matters, and find the luck in small things.

Metaphysics Insight

Today is the fifteenth day of the tenth lunar month — the Lower Origin festival 下元节, named with the Upper Origin of the fifteenth of the first month and the Middle Origin of the fifteenth of the seventh as the "Three Origins." Their root lies in the Taoist faith in the Three Officials: Taoism honors three great emperors — the Official of Heaven, the Official of Earth and the Official of Water — in the folk formula "the Heaven Official bestows blessings, the Earth Official pardons sins, the Water Official releases from adversity"; their birthdays fall on the fifteenth of the first, the seventh and the tenth months respectively, and the three fifteenths became the three festivals of Upper, Middle and Lower Origin. The Upper Origin is the gay one, lanterns and moon in one radiance; the Middle Origin the solemn one, river lamps ferrying the shades; the Lower Origin is the quietest — it is the last "Origin" of the year, and the birthday of the Water Official. Why does the Water Official belong to the tenth month? One account: the tenth month is the Hài month and Hài belongs to water; after Start of Winter the water command holds sway, and the august spirit who rules water and releases from adversity was set upon the month's fullest night. The faith in the Three Officials is unexpectedly old: the Records of the Three Kingdoms, in the commentary to the biography of Zhāng Lǔ 三国志·张鲁传, quotes the Brief Account 典略 on the Way of the Five Pecks of Rice at the end of Han and its method of prayer — "They wrote the sick person's name and the intent to confess his faults. They made three copies: one they sent up to Heaven and fastened on a mountain; one they buried in the earth; one they sank in the water. This was called the Three Officials' letter." Falling ill, one wrote down one's own name and words of repentance, in three copies: one carried up the mountain and given to Heaven, one buried in the soil and given to Earth, one sunk in the water and given to Water — Heaven, Earth and Water each received a letter, and these were the Three Officials. Read today, it is a letter of repentance posted three times — first self-examination, then release: the plainest sense the faith ever had. The customs of the Lower Origin follow the words "release from adversity": on this day offerings are made to the Water Official and to the ancestors, the temples hold fasts and altars, and in the country people steam buns of hemp paste, pound sticky cakes and wrap taro dumplings to give to kin and friends; in some northern places they also make offerings to the dead and pray for a plenteous year to come; in the old usage the day was for examining oneself and loosening the knots within, sending the year's adversities away with the water. In today's almanac the Jade Hall of the Yellow Path holds the day, and offerings and meeting family and friends stand favorable — offerings to the ancestors and a small family gathering on the Lower Origin suit the season; praying for blessings, travel and entering a house stand avoided — with the Month's Aversion at the door, vows and altars, long journeys and house moves wait for a luckier day. The Three Officials' letter: one copy to Heaven, one to Earth, one to Water. Let today's matters stay with the family table and a stick of incense — that is the best arrangement for a Lower Origin night.
