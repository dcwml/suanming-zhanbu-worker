Metaphysics Insight

Today the auspicious column holds one named Liùyí 六仪, while the baleful column stands guard with Yànduì 厌对 and Zhāoyáo 招摇 — open the old books, and these turn out to be one spirit wearing three name-tags. The Cōngjì Biànfāng Shū, quoting the Tiānbǎo Calendar, says it plainly: "Yànduì is the day the month's Repulsion stands opposed. Its day refuses marriage; and as Zhāoyáo, it refuses crossing water by boat." Yànduì is the ground opposite the month's Repulsion (the yīn establishment): the Repulsion walks the twelve branches backward — in the Hài month it sits at Chǒu, and Chǒu's opposite is precisely Wèi, so today, a Wèi day, Yànduì holds the watch. One branch, entered in three registers: written in the baleful column as "Yànduì," it refuses marriage — the Repulsion's opposition strikes at the union of yīn and yáng, so the great red rite stands aside; written again as "Zhāoyáo," it refuses crossing water — Zhāoyáo was originally the name of the seventh star of the Big Dipper, whose aspect governs storms on the waterways, borrowed as the taboo on crossings; and in the auspicious column it draws an easy posting under the name "Liùyí," with some old books saying outright that Liùyí "favors kinship pledges and presenting gifts." One star: here refusing marriage, there favoring betrothal — is this not self-contradiction? The Cōngjì Biànfāng Shū admits the muddled account itself and merely "keeps both sayings on record" — the old date-selection schools each handed down their own teaching, and one spirit with two names, its favors and refusals reversed, is no solitary case; the official calendar enters both sayings together and lets the reader weigh which to follow. Carried out to today's almanac, the old hands' shrewdness in bookkeeping a contradiction comes clear: the auspicious column takes in "Liùyí," so betrothal pledges and proposal gifts open in the favorable column — proposing, presenting gifts, kinship pledges walk straight through; the baleful column takes in "Yànduì," so marriage locks into the avoided column — the wedding procession and the bows at the hall stand aside today. One joyous matter split into two steps: the first walks Liùyí's favorable face, the second avoids Yànduì's baleful one — one star's name-tag written on both sides, each column taking what it needs: this is not the almanac quarreling with itself, but a living specimen of the old arts' "one spirit, many names; both sayings kept" — and today is a rare open page.
