Metaphysics Insight

Today's almanac holds an apparent contradiction: "marriage ceremonies" stands squarely in the favored column, while "betrothal proposals" and "engagement pledges" sit in the avoided one — all matters of marriage, so why is one favored and the others shunned? The answer lies in the Six Rites, each with its proper time. Classical weddings unfolded in six steps: the proposal (Nǎcǎi), the inquiry after names (Wènmíng), the divination and notification (Nàjí), the betrothal gifts (Nàzhēng), the fixing of the date (Qǐngqī) and the bridal procession (Qīnyíng). The proposal opens the suit; an engagement pledge is the binding of the match; the marriage ceremony is the wedding itself — and in the date-selector's eyes these are three different creatures. A wedding consummates an established bond, and so prizes completion: today's Triple Harmony, Heavenly Grace and Golden Hall answer precisely to that image. But a betrothal forges a new contract, the very thing least tolerant of reversal — the Eight Speciality blurs stems and branches into one undifferentiated qi, and Gouchen drags matters into dispute; to sign a new pledge on such a day invites entanglement, so it is expressly avoided. One favored, one avoided — and there lies the almanac's fine discrimination: not a blanket "weddings auspicious," but proposal, pledge and procession each assigned their proper day. Folk custom still echoes it: choose an "engagement day" for the betrothal and a "wedding day" for the procession — two celebrations, two dates, each rite at its own hour.
