Marriage ceremonies · Moving into a house · Setting the bed · Travel

On Gēng Zǐ day the Gēng metal stem sits upon Zǐ water — white metal over clear water, lucidity within the season's chill. Today is the Autumn Equinox itself: the sun stands above the equator, day and night divide evenly, cold and heat strike their balance, and yin and yang each hold their perfect half. Sīmìng keeps the Yellow Path with seven auspicious spirits in train — Yuèdé, Shídé, Yángdé, Mínrì, Yùyǔ, Sīmìng and Míngfèiduì — yet seven inauspicious spirits — Hékuí, Sǐshén, Tiānlì, Zhìsǐ, Sìjì, Jiǔhǔ and Wǎngwáng — arrive in equal number, and fortune and misfortune cancel into a day of quiet consolidation: sacrificial rites answer the old custom of saluting the moon at the equinox, while bathing, decorating walls and leveling roads are the homely work of putting one's surroundings in order — all else best avoided. Each of the seven carries its counsel: Wǎngwáng bars journeys afar, hence travel stands in the taboos; Sǐshén and Zhìsǐ are unkind to life's great occasions, so marriage ceremonies, moving into a house and setting the bed should wait — on this hinge of day and night, keeping indoors and tending one's own courtyard is to move with the season.

Zodiac Fortune · Rat 鼠

Today's earthly branch is Zǐ, which corresponds to the Rat — making the Rat the zodiac protagonist of the day. On a Gēng Zǐ day metal and water breed one another, and the Rat presides over its own branch, attended by Sīmìng of the Yellow Path and the auspices of Yuèdé, Shídé and Yángdé; yet with seven inauspicious spirits standing opposite, the watchword today is "keep still": as the equinox splits yin and yang down the middle, better to learn the Rat's art of storing and resting than to press for gains. Sacrificial rites and bathing stand in the favors: a morning bath and a stick of incense accord with the old equinox homage to the moon; decorating walls and leveling roads are favored too — mending the walls, tidying the house and smoothing the path before one's door are the day's proper work of settling body and mind. Marriage ceremonies, moving into a house and setting the bed stand in the taboos: with Sǐshén and Zhìsǐ on duty, life's great occasions should wait for another lucky date; Wǎngwáng especially forbids journeys, so travel and business trips are best deferred — above all toward the South, where the Sha lies. The day clashes with the Horse and the Sha lies South: those born in the Year of the Horse should keep a low profile in all matters and refrain from quarrels with Rats. The Wealth God sits East, the Fortune God Southwest and the Joy God Northwest — directions worth noting, yet today is no day for ventures and trade; keeping what one has is the wiser course — two days hence comes the Mid-Autumn moon, so rest the spirit and await the reunion.

Metaphysics Insight

Today is the Autumn Equinox. The ancients counted the days in pentads of five, and the equinox keeps its own triptych: "First, thunder ceases its voice; second, the hibernating insects plaster their doors; third, the waters begin to run low." As yin gathers strength after the equinox, thunder — the voice of summer — withdraws from the sky; sensing the cold, the small creatures seal their burrow mouths with fine clay, leaving only a thread of air, ready to sleep through the winter; and as the rainy season closes, rainfall thins, rivers and lakes subside, and some marshes begin to dry. Read together, the three pentads draw a single picture of heaven and earth drawing inward: thunder exits, insects shut their doors, waters recede — all things turn from outward display to inward storing. The almanac speaks the same language today: favors for sacrificial rites, bathing, mending walls and leveling roads; taboos on travel, moving house and setting beds — everywhere the wisdom of drawing in, in quiet accord with the season's signs. Since 2018 the Autumn Equinox has also carried a newer name in China: the Chinese Farmers' Harvest Festival — for the equinox finds the five grains going home to the granary, and the day's even split of light and dark is like a set of scales, a fair weighing of the year's labor; the old phrase "evenly dividing the autumn colors" tells precisely of this poised, balanced beauty.
