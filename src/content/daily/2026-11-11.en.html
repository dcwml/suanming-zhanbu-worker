On a Jǐ Chǒu day, Jǐ is yin earth — earth of gardens and tillage; Chǒu is the cold soil of deep winter, hiding within it jǐ, guǐ and xīn — two earths stacked together, an image of weight and keeping. The day god in the twelve-spirits round is Yùtáng 玉堂, the Jade Hall — a Yellow Path auspicious day: the Jade Hall governs documents, celebrations and feasts, and with the White Tiger stepping down yesterday and the Jade Hall taking watch today, the keynote turns from moving to resting. Ten auspicious spirits face five inauspicious ones, a lineup seldom seen: Yuèdélé 月德合 heads the list — the gathering of noble helpers, every ill quietly dissolving; Liùhé 六合 and Wǔhé 五合 arrive together, a double harmony governing the joy of reunion — meeting family and friends on the favorable list answers exactly to it; Wǔfù 五富 governs abundance of wealth and Jīntáng 金堂 the peace of the house — installing doors and setting up the bed both stand favorable, and that is today's theme: where the body is to settle, first settle the door, then the bed. Yet the inauspicious camp holds its pressers too: Sǐshén 死神 and Hékuí 河魁 govern killing air, and the great rite of marriage still avoids today — Bùjiàng 不将 is expressly the marriage spirit and stands plainly in the auspicious list, yet the avoided column still writes marriages, precisely because Death's Spirit presses the line. Yóuhuò 游祸, the wandering calamity, rules out prayer and offering; Wǔxū 五虚 governs empty wasting and Tiānláo 天牢 the fetter — of the twelve avoided matters, the moving affairs of travel, relocation, moving house, breaking ground and raising beams all stand adverse. The seven favorable matters are all small ones of ordering: consecrating vessels, cutting garments, trimming hair, weaving nets, setting beds, meeting old friends — a day that forbids moving crowds calls instead for rest and setting in order, and the give-and-take is plain. The Na Yin Thunderbolt Fire holds its post a second day — thunder hidden in cloud: yesterday answering movement, today answering stillness; on a clear auspicious Jade Hall day, gathering strength in quiet is the better course. Clash the Goat, the Sha lies East: Chǒu and Wèi stand opposed — keep clear of contention and partnership with Goat people, and let eastern journeys take a slower step.

Zodiac Fortune · Ox 牛

Today's earthly branch is Chǒu, which corresponds to the Ox — making the Ox the protagonist of the day, and this is the Ox's own watch in the round of branches: in the deep hours of Chǒu, past one and before three in the morning, the Ox takes the night shift. The Jade Hall rides in on the Yellow Path with Yuèdélé 月德合 anchoring the day: documents and celebrations go smoothly for the Ox today, and when difficulties rise, help arrives quietly — negotiations, signings and job interviews may all be taken with a steady hand. The double harmony of Liùhé 六合 and Wǔhé 五合 favors meeting family and friends — the day's main melody for the Ox is harmony among people: reuniting with old friends, small family gatherings, mending an old rift, all in season. Jīntáng 金堂 keeps the house at peace, and installing doors and setting up the bed both stand favorable — put the dwelling in order, change the bedding, mend the door, all most auspicious; when the house settles, the mind settles with it. Wǔfù 五富 governs abundance of wealth — take stock, sort the accounts, count what is already yours; with no market-opening or trading on the favorable list, guarding wealth beats venturing it. Haircutting and tailoring garments stand favorable too — before meeting clients or interviewers, put your own front in order; the image is part of the fortune. Tiānláo 天牢 stands in the list: keep one extra guard against quarrels, disputes and legal tangles — read contract clauses word by word, keep official matters by the book, and step clear of litigation. Travel, relocation and breaking ground are avoided: far journeys and moving house may wait. Clash the Goat, the Sha lies East: Chǒu and Wèi stand opposed — avoid partnerships and quarrels with Goat people, and let eastern journeys take a slower step. In the body, Jǐ earth answers the spleen and stomach, and Chǒu is the damp cold soil of deep winter, when chill most easily burdens the spleen — keep food warm, soft and gentle, and chew as the Ox chews its cud, slow and fine; sleep early, for in the Chǒu hours the Ox lies in its stall chewing quietly — do not keep the late night with anyone.

Metaphysics Insight

Yesterday told how the Rat bites open heaven; today the Ox takes its watch — so let us tell how the Chǒu Ox cleaves the earth. After the Rat's bite, heaven and earth were split, yet the great land lay wild and unbroken, weeds and thickets everywhere — who plowed this wilderness into fields? In the folk's telling it is the Ox of Chǒu. One account says the Ox was a star-spirit of heaven's palace (the Ox of the Golden Ox constellation), sent down to scatter grain seed and teach the people to till; seeing the people's hardship it could not bear to return, and stayed in the dust of the world bearing the yoke, paying for the fault of "opening heaven's granary without leave" with the labor of its shoulders — and so the Ox eats grass all its life and never ceases plowing. Another says the Ox was a courier at the Jade Emperor's gate: the decree charged the people to "live by one meal in three days," but the Ox, fumbling the message, announced "three meals in one day" — the granaries of the world fell short, and the Emperor sent the Ox down to plow the land, helping the people open fields and grow grain to mend its error; from then on the Ox eats only grass. The two accounts differ, but they land in one place: the Ox broke open the wilderness by the labor of bearing loads, and so it is said — "the Zǐ Rat opens heaven, the Chǒu Ox cleaves the earth." Why is the Ox paired with the Chǒu hour? Chǒu runs from one to three after midnight: in daytime the Ox eats in haste, but at night it ruminates, chewing the cud at leisure — and the Chǒu hours are exactly when the Ox lies in its stall, turning the day's hastily swallowed coarse fare over and grinding it fine, making it its own; the ancients watched the Ox's nature so, and paired it with Chǒu. The Ox stood high in the rites of old as well: the Tàiláo 太牢 sacrifice put the Ox first — ox, sheep and pig together made the Tàiláo, which only the Son of Heaven might offer — the Ox is at once the highest offering on the altar and the most silent laborer between the furrows, one body serving both "revering heaven" and "feeding the people." And so to today's almanac: the Ox's own watch on its own day, a clear auspicious hour of the Yellow Path, favorable for resting, not for moving — exactly the virtue of the Ox: bearing loads without complaint, chewing slow and fine, gathering strength in stillness. Lie down early tonight and learn from the Ox of the Chǒu hours: take the things you swallowed in haste by day, and grind them over slowly in your dream.
