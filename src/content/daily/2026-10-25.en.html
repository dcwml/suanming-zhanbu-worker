Metaphysics Insight

Two days ago the Azure Dragon, yesterday the Bright Hall — today the turn falls to the third of the Yellow Path's six spirits: the Golden Casket. Kuì is the old character for the cabinet, a vessel of storing; a casket made of gold speaks of utmost sturdiness and worth. When the ancients kept what mattered most, they spoke of "golden caskets and stone chambers": the Book of Han records that the court's oaths and covenants with its meritorious lords were to be "kept in the ancestral temple, in golden caskets and stone chambers" — gold-locked cabinets within stone vaults, proof against fire, damp and theft, the highest standard of safekeeping that age could devise. The sky-spirit takes its image from exactly this: the Golden Casket is the treasury-star of heaven, where wealth and treasures are stored, governing keeping and riches — hence on a Golden Casket day of the Yellow Path, nothing suits better than the treasury's own affairs: today opening granaries, releasing wealth and goods and acquiring property all stand in the favored column, the very picture of the treasury-spirit on duty and the vault doors unbarred. Worth noting, the Golden Casket is also a familiar in destiny-reading: among the year-based stars of Ziwei astrology there is a Golden Casket star, governing savings and hoarded treasure; in old tales, "books of the golden casket" meant secret, treasured classics. And there is a second finesse today: the Post Horse and the Golden Casket hold office on the same day — the Post Horse governs motion and travel, the Golden Casket governs storing and keeping, one moving and one hiding; they seem at odds, yet complete each other: the horse is on the road while the vault has a floor — the traveler seeking wealth goes out from a kept house, takes profit in motion and returns to store it: two faces of the one merchant's way. The Yellow Path six are now half told: the Azure Dragon governs sprouting, the Bright Hall command, the Golden Casket the treasury — each keeping its own stall, each to its own office; the almanac's sense of system lives in precisely this punctilious division of labor.
