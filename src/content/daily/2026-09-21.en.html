On Wù Xū day, stem and branch are both earth — solid, weighty, unmoving. Tiānláo, the Heavenly Enclosure, keeps the Black Path today, a star of tangles and delay; yet four auspicious spirits gather — Mǔcāng, Shǒurì, Jíqī and Xùshì — and the day turns fierce without danger: Mǔcāng, the Mother's Granary, nourishes and sustains, Xùshì, the Continuer of Generations, lengthens the family line — together the two are at their best for weddings and betrothals; Shǒurì, the Keeper of the Day, secures and holds, Jíqī, the Lucky Date, crowns undertakings with gladness — hence construction, groundbreaking, laying foundations, raising the ridgepole, building houses, opening a business and travel may all proceed in company. Eighteen favors stand in today's list, a rare day of many undertakings. The inauspicious spirits are but three — Yuèhài, Xuèjì and Tiānláo: Yuèhài, the Month's Harm, forbids feuds and injuries among people; Xuèjì, the Blood Taboo, bars the drawing of blood — seeking a doctor and treating illness stand in the favors, yet acupuncture and bloodletting should wait, and funerals and burial are set aside for the Blood Taboo's sake. The Autumn Equinox is but two days off, metal's vigor gathering in as earth holds the season: lay foundations, raise roofs, and settle new beginnings upon this thick earth while it lasts.

Only Xuèjì keeps its watch: seeing a doctor is fine, yet acupuncture, bloodletting and piercings should wait; sleep early to nourish the blood and go easy on spicy food. The day clashes with the Dragon and the Sha lies North: those born in the Year of the Dragon should keep a low profile in all matters, avoid long journeys north, and refrain from quarrels with Dogs.

Metaphysics Insight

Today's day pillar is Wù Xū — stem and branch both earth, yet its Na Yin belongs to wood, named "Wood on the Plain": the apparent contradiction between the two systems is the very reason to speak of the sixty Nayin. The Na Yin pairs the sixty ganzhi two by two, binding each pair to an image of the five elements: Jiǎzǐ and Yǐchǒu are Gold in the Sea, Bǐngyín and Dīngmǎo Fire in the Furnace, Wùchén and Jǐsì Wood of the Great Forest — and Wùxū with Jǐhài take Wood on the Plain. The method grows out of the ancient study of pitch-pipes: since before the Qin, the doctrine of sixty pitches "circling the palace" wove the five notes of the pentatonic scale into the ganzhi and gathered them under the five elements — hence "Na Yin": na, to take in, receiving the stem-branch into the music of the elements. Masters of fate read the stem-branch's own element as the "orthodox element", which speaks of substance, and the Na Yin as the "transformed element", which speaks of image — two systems running in parallel, each with its own office. Wù Xū is thick earth — whence a tree? The old books read the image thus: Xū is the month of late autumn when earth waxes full, and from that thick earth a tree grows of itself, leaning on no mountain, touching no water, flourishing on the flat ground — hence Wood on the Plain: the stock of rich earth put to the work of springing green, like one born to comfortable means who yet builds a fortune with their own hands — roots secure, vitality free. Today's favors run long with construction, groundbreaking, foundations, foundation stones, houses and roof ridges, echoing the very image of "raising a home from the plain": wood from the earth, thriving on what has accumulated — in the old Nayin system hides the plainest of Chinese imaginings about standing firm and growing tall.
