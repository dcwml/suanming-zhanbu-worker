On Guǐ Sì day, the day stem Guǐ (Water) is the water of gentle rain and dew, sitting upon Sì (Fire), within which hide Bǐng Fire the direct-wealth star, Wù Earth the direct-officer star and Gēng Metal the direct-seal star — wealth, officer and seal all held in reserve. The month pillar Dīng Yǒu arrives with Yǒu Metal commanding the season after White Dew; flourishing Metal generates Water, while Dīng Fire, the indirect-wealth star, stands revealed on the month stem, the wealth star bright and clear. Today the day branch Sì forms a half-combination with the month branch Yǒu, the Metal frame gathering its qi — hence the auspicious spirit Sānhé presides; by the old rule, autumn claims the Rén and Guǐ days for Sìxiàng, and today's Guǐ day stands squarely in that seat. Tiānxǐ governs joyous occasions, Tiānyī governs healing, Yuè'ēn bestows grace, Línrì favors petitions and governance, Pǔhù shields prayers and blessings, and Bùjiàng is the auspicious spirit of marriage — eight auspicious spirits arriving together, so the "Yi" column holds twelve matters led by opening market, trading, signing contracts, collecting wealth and hanging plaques. Yet Zhūquè of the Black Path presides, governing disputes of the tongue, documents, fire and theft; the Chòngrì Repeated Day also sits upon the branch — old lore calls it the day of matters repeated, hence the "Ji" column is led by marriage, with breaking ground and burial in its ranks: great ceremonies wish to be accomplished in one stroke and dread being repeated, so the almanac avoids them; travel, moving into a house, relocation, acquiring livestock and litigation are also better postponed. Overall, today favors commerce over ceremony: opening for business, signing contracts and pursuing wealth are timely, while weddings, funerals, long journeys and moving house should await a better day.

Metaphysics Insight

In the cycle of the twelve earthly branches, two days stand apart: the Sì days and the Hài days, known in the old books as Chòngrì — the Repeated Days. The Lìlì (Register of Examples) says: "The repeated days are Sì and Hài." Why these two alone? The ancients took Sì as the pole of yang, where the yang breath reaches its utmost, and Hài as the pole of yin, where the yin breath likewise reaches its utmost. But nothing extreme endures: at the pole of yang, yin is born; at the pole of yin, yang is born. Yin and yang circle back upon each other at the extreme, turning and beginning again, as if arriving at the end only to start anew — hence the name "repeated." Old lore holds that the Repeated Day presides over matters doubled: joyous occasions risk being held twice, and mourning risks returning. Great ceremonies wish to be settled in a single stroke and dread being replayed; hence almanacs, meeting a Repeated Day, list marriage, breaking ground and burial among the taboos. Today, Guǐ Sì, is precisely such a day — the "Ji" column led by marriage, with breaking ground and burial in its ranks, in perfect accord with the old rule. Curiously, though Sì and Hài are counted among the inauspicious spirits, they are also a philosophical footnote to "the end that begins again" — yang at its utmost births yin, yin at its utmost births yang, turning without cease, the very "way of return" of the Yijing. The ancients wrote their reverence for "the extreme that reverses" into a single column of taboos in the almanac.
