The equinox is past and the metal qi ripens: climb the tower, admire the moon and gather the family close — that is today's proper business above all.

Metaphysics Insight

Yesterday's equinox piece mentioned the phrase "evening sacrifice to the moon at the Autumn Equinox" — for the moon was worshipped, by ancient ordinance, on the equinox itself. The Book of Rites records that the Son of Heaven "salutes the sun in spring and the moon in autumn": sunrise belongs to yang, moonrise to yin, and the equinox rites to sun and moon were grand state ceremonies from the earliest dynasties onward. But the equinox is a solar term, fixed by the sun's course, and it falls on no fixed lunar date: some years it lands on a moonless first night, some years on a thin crescent — to hold a moon sacrifice with no moon in sight always left something wanting. So the people moved that reverence to the one night in the eighth month when the moon rounds to its fullest — the fifteenth: near the equinox, the metal qi at its height, osmanthus scent through the streets. Thus the Mid-Autumn moon-offering took shape, and the emperors' ancient rite of evening moon passed into every ordinary household. The full-moon night carries its own counsel in the almanac's reckoning: the fifteenth lunar phase is called Wàng, the Full Moon, when the sun sets west as the moon rises east, the two luminaries pulling against each other from opposite horizons — the ancients held that on such days tempers run high and spirits float, and so a Full Moon day in the old almanac comes down to one word, "stillness": favor quiet reunion, avoid long roads and great undertakings. Today Qīnglóng keeps the Yellow Path, first among the twelve spirits: the Azure Dragon belongs to the wood of the east, lords of celebration and noble patrons, head of the six Yellow Path guardians. The Black Path's counsel of stillness and the Yellow Path's promise of joy meet and agree on this one night — lay the reunion table, light the moon-offering incense, and heaven's timing and human affairs suit one another perfectly: the gentlest page in the ancestors' art of choosing the day.
