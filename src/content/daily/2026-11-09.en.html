On a Dīng Hài day, Dīng fire sits upon Hài water — a candle carried to the water's edge, fire come into the water country; Dīng fire reaches Hài as its embryo stage, its flame carried but not yet kindled, so the day master wears a look of gathering-in by nature, and the Rén water hidden in Hài secretly draws toward Dīng — the officer star holds affection, keeping what one has is well served, but rash ventures are not. The day's spirit in the twelve-spirits round is Tiāndé, the Heavenly Virtue — a Yellow Path auspicious day: the Heavenly Virtue is heaven's own grace, the shelter of the high, dissolving calamity and lifting misfortune, and worship with prayer is exactly timely — and Bǎoguāng, the Treasured Light, standing in the auspicious list, is the Heavenly Virtue's Yellow Path by-name, whose image is "light made manifest." Yet the month pillar is Jǐ Hài and the day pillar Dīng Hài — two Hài stacked upon each other — and when the day's branch answers the month's branch in the same breath, the day is "Established," the Monthly Established holding command: royal qi enthroned, in season and in power, so everything is fit for reverence and unfit for stirring, and the favored column keeps only offerings and bathing, surrendering all other matters to the ban. The three auspicious spirits guard "reverence" and "continuance" alone: Wángrì, the King's Day, is where the season's royal qi resides — winter's king is Hài — favoring sacrifice and the calling upon the esteemed; Xùshì, the World Continuing, governs the line of generations unbroken and incense never going cold, favoring prayer and the prayer for heirs; Bǎoguāng governs light made manifest — not one among them is a spirit of founding or of moving. Eight inauspicious spirits press the lists: Yuèjiàn, Xiǎoshí and Yuèxíng hold command and hold punishment, and every work of stirring offends their edge; Tǔfǔ, the Bureau of Earth, keeps the ground, barring all breaking of earth and digging of wells; Jiǔkǎn and Jiǔjiāo, the Nine Pitfalls and the Nine Burnings, govern entrapment and scorching — climbing high, crossing water and going by boat may wait; Xuèjì, the Blood Taboo, forbids the letting of blood — acupuncture, surgery and the knife's edge set one day aside; and Chóngrì, the Double Day, arrives — for Sì and Hài are the doubled days — where fortune doubles and ill doubles too: raise an ill work upon it and it may be done twice over, so let it not be raised at all. The avoided column writes "none," yet this is not a day fit for all things — the four words "all other matters set aside" are already the ban upon the world. Today is the first of the tenth lunar month, the Cold Clothing Festival: under the Heavenly Virtue's care, with offerings standing favored — sending winter clothes and remembering the ancestors is exactly timely. Clash the Snake, the Sha lies West: Sì and Hài stand opposed — keep clear of contention and partnership with Snake people, and let western journeys take a slower step.

Metaphysics Insight

Today is the first day of the tenth lunar month — the Cold Clothing Festival, ranked with Qingming and the Ghost Festival (Zhōngyuán) among the three great days of remembrance, and called in folk speech the "Tenth Month's Morning." Its source is an old farming verse: the Book of Odes, in the airs of Bīn, sings "in the seventh month the Fire Star descends, in the ninth month robes are given" — robes are readied in the ninth month and worn as the tenth begins; and the Rites' Records, in the calendar of the first winter month, notes that "the Son of Heaven first wears furs" — at winter's threshold even the Son of Heaven changes into fur. The giving of robes is a rite older than memory, and by the Song dynasty it reached from the living to the dead: the Dongjing Meng Hua Lu records that on the first of the tenth month "the ministers and below receive robes," the court granting brocade jackets to its officials; and when the living added clothes, thought turned to the ancestors underground with no garment against the cold — so paper clothes were burned and the act was named "sending winter clothes." The Ming's Di Jing Jing Wu Lüe puts it plainly: "On the first day of the tenth month the paper shops cut paper in five colors, making garments of men and women, a foot long or so, called winter clothes" — cut, folded into coat, trouser, boot and cap, and burned before the grave, as the proverb runs: "on the tenth month's first, send winter clothes." Behind the festival stands one of the deepest of all remembered loves: the tale tells that in the Qin time Mèng Jiāngnǚ carried winter clothes a thousand li to her husband Fàn Qǐliáng, conscripted to build the Great Wall; arriving, she learned he had died and his bones lay packed into the wall, and her three days of wailing broke eight hundred li of it — and ever since, the three words "sending winter clothes" have carried a warmth passed from mouth to heart. The folk customs keep their fine measures: winter clothes should be burned before the sun goes down, so the ancestors may gather them in the light; the ash is drawn into a circle with an opening facing the grave or the old home, while clothes for the wandering lonely are burned outside the circle, in a place apart — and in every measure runs the old sense of "inside and outside kept distinct, near and far kept in order." And so to today's almanac: yesterday, the thirtieth of the ninth month, the dark of the moon sent off poverty; today, the first of the tenth, the new moon sends winter clothes — one word "sending" carried over two days. The Heavenly Virtue on the Yellow Path, offerings standing favored — the almanac and the festival speak one sentence today: reverence before all other matters, and a cup of wine, a set of paper clothes, one bathed and cleansed pair of hands is the oldest answer to the word "home."
