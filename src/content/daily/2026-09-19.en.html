Inauspicious Spirits

Yóuhuò 游祸 · Xuèzhī 血支 · Dìnáng 地囊 · Wǔlí 五离 · Báihǔ 白虎

Praying for blessings · Consecration · Opening market · Moving into house · Groundbreaking

On Bǐng Shēn day, the sun's fire sits upon autumn metal — White Dew has passed and metal commands the season, the sun drawing in its flame as it shines upon ore, its brilliance held within. Báihǔ, the White Tiger, presides on the Black Path: an inauspicious star of wounds and blood; yet six auspicious spirits arrive together — Wángrì, Tiānmǎ, Wǔfù, Shèngxīn, Chúshén and Míngfèi — and their strength is enough to stand against it: Míngfèi, the crowing rooster and barking dog, ministers to the affairs of burial, so interment, reopening graves, erecting tombstones, repairing graves and preparing coffin timber are all timely; Wǔfù, the Five Abundances, fills the storehouse of wealth, making the acquiring and herding of livestock seasonable; Chúshén, the Spirit of Removal, presides over bathing and cleansing, so cleansing rites and sweeping the house also stand in the favors. Yet Yóuhuò, Xuèzhī, Dìnáng and Wǔlí all appear beside: on a Shēn day, Wǔlí is the Parting of Sun and Moon — metal's killing cool means parting, and bargaining and signing are the business of parting, so opening market is barred; Dìnáng, the Sack of the Earth, keeps the earth spirits on duty, so moving into a house and groundbreaking must be avoided; prayers and consecration, too, should wait upon a Black Path day. Three days before the Autumn Equinox, metal's vigor nears its fullness and turn: let all things gather and draw in — to move with the season is itself the seeking of good fortune.

Zodiac Fortune · Monkey 猴

Today's earthly branch is Shēn, which corresponds to the Monkey — making the Monkey the zodiac protagonist of the day. Monkeys preside over their own branch and gain its vigor: quick of thought and decisive in act. Though Báihǔ rides the Black Path, it neither clashes nor punishes the Monkey's own branch — for those born in the Year of the Monkey, this is a day for sorting and renewing: cleansing rites, bathing and sweeping the house stand in the favors, an hour to clear what has piled up and wash the old away; installing doors, making roof beams and raising scaffolding are all affairs of door and timber, fitting for mending the house and setting one's things in order. In wealth, Wǔfù keeps the storehouse full: acquiring property and setting savings aside will hold their gains — only opening market is taboo: collect what is owed, manage and store, but do not open a shop or expose wealth. Báihǔ governs wounds and Xuèzhī the sight of blood: be careful driving, with blades and at heights today, and refrain from quarrels. The day clashes with the Tiger and the Sha lies South: those born in the Year of the Tiger should keep a low profile in all matters, avoid long journeys south, and refrain from quarrels with Monkeys; with the Joy God and Wealth God both in the Southwest, take negotiations and money matters to the southwestern quarter, and the Fortune God sits Northwest — calls upon elders and patrons in the northwest will go smoothly.

Metaphysics Insight

Among today's inauspicious spirits stand the two characters Wǔlí — the Five Departures — so let us speak of this company of parting. The Shūyàolǐ, the Essential Calendar, says: "Wǔlí are the month's spirits of departure; on such days, no marrying, no gathering with kin and friends, no bargaining, no signing of contracts." The Lìlì points to their seat: "Wǔlí are the Shēn and Yǒu days." The ancients matched the ten stems in turn to the branches Shēn and Yǒu and obtained five pairs, preserved in a folk rhyme: Jiǎ Shēn and Yǐ Yǒu part Heaven and Earth (天地离); Bǐng Shēn and Dīng Yǒu part Sun and Moon (日月离); Wù Shēn and Jǐ Yǒu part the People (人民离); Gēng Shēn and Xīn Yǒu part Metal and Stone (金石离); Rén Shēn and Guǐ Yǒu part the Rivers (江河离). If Heaven, Earth, sun, moon, people, metal, stone and the rivers themselves may part, the bonds and gatherings of human affairs are hardly to be fixed upon such days. Why Shēn and Yǒu? Both belong to metal, strongest in autumn; metal's air is the killing cool of harvest — leaves yellow and fall, wild geese turn south: images of parting, every one. Cáo Zhènguī offers another reading: "Wǔlí are the hours when yin and yang meet a second time — to join again is to part." The Five Departures stand opposite the Five Unions (五合) of the Yín and Mǎo days: union carried to its extreme turns to parting — once more, things reverse at their extreme. Today is Bǐng Shēn, the very Parting of Sun and Moon: sun and moon draw apart, their lights unjoined — hence prayers, consecration and opening market all stand in the taboos. Yet the Five Departures have another face: the Yùlì Kǎoyuán cites the tongshu — Shēn and Yǒu, standing opposite the Five Unions, are also named Chúshén, the Spirit of Removal, and their days are fitting for bathing and washing away misfortune. The taboo of parting, turned over, becomes the use of cleansing. Today's auspicious list carries Chúshén, and the favors hold bathing, cleansing rites and sweeping the house — the very sense of it: old grime may be washed, old matters may be closed; riding autumn's killing cool to bid the old farewell and gather in, here is the turn for good hidden in an ill-starred day.
